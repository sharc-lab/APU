\begin{abstract}
When a local agent's conversation outgrows the context window, Ollama trims it and still reports success, while a stock llama-server at the same small window fails loudly, with an HTTP error on every wrong task turn% reg: xruntime-llamacpp-wrong-rate
. We measure what is lost with pre-registered experiments on an AMD APU, an NVIDIA laptop GPU and an Intel laptop. At windows of 16384 tokens and below, no session of three 8B to 14B models kept its full history% reg: R2-real-v1b-survival
, and models up to 70B lose history once the window is exceeded% reg: H-scale-verdict | H-scale-family-verdict
. Rule loss appears in the 8B and 14B models (5 seeds) and in qwen3:32b (2 of 3 sessions at 4096)% reg: H-scale-verdict
, and not in llama3.3:70b or mistral-small3.2:24b (0 of 3 each)% reg: H-scale-verdict | H-scale-family-verdict
. These cross-model results are R2 rule-following evidence, not B3 task accuracy. On a tool-using task workload (B3), where qwen3:14b is the one model that passed validation, its wrong-task rate rises from 11.3\% (17/150) at its default window to 42.0\% (63/150) at 4096 on Ollama, and every wrong task is silent% reg: x2-evalset-tasks-real-v2-wrong-rate
; on llama-server the 4096 effect replicates (+92.0 points, 95\% CI +88.7 to +94.7) but no wrong task is silent% reg: xruntime-llamacpp-wrong-rate
. Runtime defaults decide how often this happens: an unmodified install loaded a 4096-token window on an Intel CPU % reg: t2s-r2-real-cpu
and 32768 on the same laptop's integrated GPU% reg: t2s-r2-real-igpu
, and the GPU backend alone changed whether llama3.1:8b wrote tool calls as text% reg: x2-r2-llama-val-32k-vulkan | blade-r2-gate-v2 | x2-r2-llama-val-32k-offload
. The fixes are partial: KV-cache quantization cut session time by 65\% to 85\% (139.4 of 214.8 and 37.1 of 43.7 min per session, 3 sessions per setting) with no measured loss of rules or recall% reg: cost-cut-config-time | b5-fa-control | b5-kv-recall-f16-rerun
, while client-side trimming and \texttt{num\_keep} each repair only part of the loss.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:4.1}

% Convention as in 4.3: measured numbers carry their register id; design constants carry "design: <source file>".

Runtimes such as Ollama and llama.cpp serve open-weight models of 8B to 70B parameters on laptops and small APU workstations, and local agents drive them for tens of turns, appending every tool result to a conversation that only grows inside a fixed context window. When the conversation outgrows it, the runtime, not the agent, decides what to remove, and on Ollama it does so silently: the request still returns HTTP 200% reg: blade-k1-layer-split
; llama-server at the same window fails loudly instead, with HTTP errors% reg: xruntime-llamacpp-wrong-rate
. The answer is well formed, and the model does not mention the loss (0/960 % reg: self-report-truncation-awareness
self-reports in our probes mentioned truncation).

\textbf{Why run locally, and what it costs.} Agent tasks are expensive in the cloud because every step re-sends the whole conversation. Priced on public agent traces (not executed, with token counts recalibrated to the cloud tokenizer), a median task of a long-horizon coding-agent set costs USD 2.5307 on a mid-tier cloud model and USD 0.1354 on a small one with realistic caching% reg: cloud-cost-per-real-task
. Our own tool-using tasks are short: run fully in the cloud, the small and mid-tier models were correct on 87/90 and 90/90 tasks % reg: all-cloud-b3-quality
at a mean USD 0.00067 and USD 0.01020 per task% reg: all-cloud-b3-cost
. Running the same tasks locally on qwen3:14b at its default window removes the bill but not the risk: 80/90 tasks were correct, and nothing marked the 10 that were not% reg: hybrid-savings-v1
. The runtime's own window-overflow flag does not find them either (Section~\ref{sec:4.7p}). A perfect detector would reach 90/90 by escalating 10 tasks% reg: hybrid-savings-v1
, but that is a ceiling, and no such detector exists; fixing a wrong answer afterwards in the cloud fixed 84.8\% (167/197) % reg: cloud-redo-full-context
at a mean of USD 0.0227 per task% reg: cloud-redo-full-context
, but a plain redo cannot undo actions already executed (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}).

A motivating case shows how easily a user lands in this regime. On an Intel laptop, an unmodified Ollama install served two models with a 4096 % reg: t2s-r2-real-cpu
token window, because device detection drops the integrated GPU and falls back to the CPU% reg: x2-device-detect-mechanism
, and every agent session then failed silently; with the integrated GPU enabled, none failed after the window (Section~\ref{sec:4.5}). Nobody chose 4096; the runtime did.

This paper asks what a local agent loses when its conversation exceeds the context window, whether anything tells the user, and what decides how often it happens. We answer with pre-registered experiments on an AMD APU, an NVIDIA laptop GPU and an Intel laptop (CPU and integrated GPU), spanning unified and discrete memory. First, on Ollama history loss is silent and appears in every model we tested, from 8B to 70B% reg: H-scale-verdict | H-scale-family-verdict
. Second, rule loss is model-dependent, and we do not claim it in general; these first two parts are R2 rule-following evidence across models, not B3 task accuracy. Third, the cost is concrete: on the B3 task workload, where qwen3:14b is the one model that passed validation, its share of tasks ending in a wrong state rises from 11.3\% (17/150) at the default window to 42.0\% (63/150) at 4096, every wrong task silent on Ollama% reg: x2-evalset-tasks-real-v2-wrong-rate
. Fourth, the runtime's defaults and backend decide how often this happens. Fifth, the fixes are partial.

We make four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{A cross-vendor map of where memory runs out and how it fails} (Section~\ref{sec:4.4}). On the Intel integrated GPU a model start succeeds or fails at one shared-heap limit that also counts host-visible buffers, and a first-principles accounting of weights, KV cache and compute predicts the measured flip point to within -257 to -26 tokens% reg: A-24-flip-point-prediction
.

\item \textbf{Runtime policy, not hardware, sets the effective window, and on Ollama overflow is silent} (Section~\ref{sec:4.5}). The same runtime gives llama3.1:8b a default window of 4096 tokens on the Intel laptop and 131072 on the AMD APU% reg: T2S-vs-X2-default-ctx
, and answers an over-length prompt with success after keeping num\_ctx/2 + 2 tokens% reg: ollama-overflow-keeps-half
.

\item \textbf{The consequences for agents, with their mechanism} (Section~\ref{sec:4.6}). On Ollama, silent failures after the window grow as the window shrinks; Ollama drops whole old messages but keeps the system prompt, and at the smallest window llama.cpp's context shift and a token cut discard it% reg: R2-mechanism-lowlevel
.

\item \textbf{Pre-registered fixes, with their limits, and a characterization of the runtime's shift rule} (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}). Client-side trimming is refuted as a general fix on two vendors% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-verdict | t2s-r2-mitigation-cpu
; \texttt{num\_keep} lowers qwen3:14b's wrong-task rate at 4096 to 30.0\% (45/150) without removing the excess% reg: x2-evalset-tasks-real-v2-wrong-rate
; a router to a cloud model is refuted on the AMD platform% reg: b5-verdict
, where a quantized KV cache was on the frontier instead% reg: b5-policy-metrics
, while under a simulated window cap the router joins the frontier beside client trimming% reg: b5-sim-constraint
; and the runtime's own context-shift rule, transcribed from the llama.cpp source, matches every observed shift in the sessions it was checked on (misses 0% reg: R2-shift-predictor-confusion
); we characterize it to explain system-prompt loss, not as a detector, and it does not predict wrong answers.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:4.2}

% Every citation below is a placeholder to verify; see "References (to verify)" at the end of this file.

\textbf{Context windows in local runtimes.} llama.cpp allocates a KV cache for a fixed number of tokens, n\_ctx, when a model loads. When a sequence would grow past it, the server can perform a \emph{context shift}: it keeps the first n\_keep tokens, discards a block of the tokens after them and continues. Ollama serves models through a bundled llama.cpp server and adds two layers. Its chat endpoint drops whole messages from the front of an over-length conversation while keeping the system message% reg: R2-mechanism-verdict
, and it starts the server with context shift enabled and a keep of 4 tokens, so an over-length prompt succeeds after keeping num\_ctx/2 + 2 tokens% reg: ollama-overflow-keeps-half
. The request option \texttt{num\_keep} sets the kept prefix, which matters only on the shift and cut paths. If the client sends no \texttt{num\_ctx}, Ollama chooses the window from the detected device (Section~\ref{sec:4.5}). None of these steps is reported to the client.

\textbf{Long-context behaviour.} Lost in the Middle~\cite{liu2024lost} finds the middle of a long input used worse than its ends; RULER~\cite{hsieh2024ruler} and LongBench~\cite{bai2024longbench} find effective context shorter than the advertised window; and multi-turn~\cite{laban2025lost} and rule-following benchmarks~\cite{zhou2023ifeval}~\cite{mu2023rules} measure how well a model uses or obeys its input. All assume the model receives the whole input; we study what the runtime removes before that.

\textbf{KV-cache management.} StreamingLLM~\cite{xiao2024streamingllm} keeps initial attention-sink tokens plus a recent window, much as a context shift keeps n\_keep tokens; H2O~\cite{zhang2023h2o} and SnapKV~\cite{li2024snapkv} evict by attention; KIVI~\cite{liu2024kivi} and KVQuant~\cite{hooper2024kvquant} quantize the cache; LLMLingua~\cite{jiang2023llmlingua} compresses the prompt. These are designed policies evaluated at fixed memory; we measure what shipped runtimes discard by default, and treat the runtime's built-in KV quantization as one context policy among several (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}).

\textbf{Agents, routing and verification.} AgentBench~\cite{liu2024agentbench}, tau-bench~\cite{yao2024taubench}, function-calling evaluations~\cite{patil2024gorilla} and SWE-bench~\cite{jimenez2024swebench} score task success, usually with hosted models and large windows; we vary the window under a fixed model, runtime and seed. Routers~\cite{routellm}~\cite{ding2024hybrid} pick a small or a large model from a learned estimate of difficulty, cascades~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} escalate when a scorer distrusts the cheap answer, and self-verification~\cite{weng2023selfverification} and sampling-consistency checks~\cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} let a model test its own answer. All need a signal that correlates with a wrong answer. The runtime's own over-window signal, which we characterize and do not contribute, is blind to wrong answers produced inside the window (Section~\ref{sec:4.7p}).

\textbf{Edge-cloud delegation and model fit.} EdgeWise~\cite{shi2026edgewise} learns which steps of a long agent task to delegate to a cloud model; EC2-Bench~\cite{huang2026ec2bench} benchmarks edge-cloud collaboration strategies; Minions~\cite{narayan2025minions} has a cloud model decompose tasks for an on-device model; Arch-Router~\cite{tran2025archrouter} routes by user-defined preferences; and llmfit~\cite{llmfit} estimates which models fit a machine's memory. An open runtime issue~\cite{ollama14259} reports that chat-history truncation is silent. None of these measures how the window a runtime loads changes an agent's correctness.

\textbf{Inference on edge hardware.} PagedAttention~\cite{kwon2023vllm} manages KV memory on servers; MELT~\cite{laskaridis2024melt}, PowerInfer~\cite{song2024powerinfer} and LLM in a flash~\cite{edge_llm_serving} characterize or accelerate inference on phones, consumer GPUs and flash-backed devices for throughput, memory and energy. We add behavioural correctness under each runtime's memory and context policy.

\section{Platforms, Harness and Methodology}\label{sec:4.3}

% Convention: measured numbers carry their register id; harness design constants carry "design: <source file>".

\textbf{Platforms and runtimes.} We study three consumer platforms from three GPU vendors: an AMD Ryzen AI Max+ (Strix Halo) APU whose Radeon iGPU shares unified memory with the CPU; a laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 4070 Laptop GPU and 8188 MiB % reg: blade-k1-layer-split
of discrete VRAM; and an Intel Core Ultra (Panther Lake) laptop with an Arc iGPU on unified memory. Ollama's device detection drops the Intel iGPU and falls back to the CPU% reg: x2-device-detect-mechanism
, so we measure Intel twice: on the CPU, as shipped, and on the iGPU, enabled only in the environment of the job's own server process. All agent runs use Ollama; the AMD and NVIDIA runs share one pinned release and the model files. Versions and drivers are listed in Table~\ref{tab:platforms}.

\textbf{The R2 agent harness.} A session is a seeded, deterministic agent conversation of 40 turns% reg: R2-v1b-qwen3-8b-reversal
. The system prompt carries five % reg: design: harness/x2_r2_agent.py
checkable rules (JSON answer with fixed keys, call a \texttt{log\_event} tool, never print a forbidden token, lengths in metres, end with a session code), a two-tool schema and recall facts. Each turn is two % reg: design: harness/x2_r2_agent.py
calls: on the first the model emits tool calls and the harness simulates results with a long filler payload that grows the context as real tool output does; the second asks for the final answer. Temperature is 0% reg: design: harness/x2_r2_agent.py
. Canaries in the system prompt and history tags in the first user message, which a message-level trim drops first, detect lost context; each canary turn asks for a new pair, so a model cannot copy a reused canary from its own earlier answer% reg: R2-validation-baseline
. A miss counts as truncation only while the final prompt exceeds the context Ollama reports as loaded (\texttt{/api/ps}, read every turn), never by the runtime's prompt count. All R2 runs withhold tools on the final call (the v1 protocol), because with tools offered there llama3.1:8b often answered with a tool call and no text; Section~\ref{sec:4.6a} repeats the main contrast with tools on every call.

\textbf{Validation gates.} Before any real run, each model passes a validation at a window where nothing is truncated, with gates fixed in advance. A rule is \emph{in use} for a model only if the model follows it on at least 90\% % reg: x2-evalset-val-v2-gates
of baseline turns; rules below the bar are dropped, not repaired, because the failures are real behaviour. For llama3.1:8b only rules 1, 3 and 4 % reg: R2-validation-v2-baseline
are in use; qwen3:14b kept all five. Negative and positive controls and a task-tool gate complete the list, and a real job refuses to start unless its model's validation passes.

\textbf{Gated silent failure.} For each session we take the first turn on which a gated metric fails. The failure is \emph{silent} when no HTTP error or error field appeared on any earlier turn, and \emph{after-window} when it falls at or after the first turn whose prompt exceeded the loaded window; otherwise it is \emph{baseline-type}, the model's own error rate rather than context loss. The window effect is the \emph{excess over default}: a tier's after-window rate minus the Ollama default tier's, with a 95\% percentile bootstrap CI over sessions (10000 % reg: R2-v1b-excess-over-default
resamples, stratified by tier and model). A 0/n or n/n cell has no bootstrap variance, so its CI understates uncertainty at five seeds per cell% reg: R2-real-v1b-first-events
; one cell was later extended to 10 seeds % reg: R2-qwen3-8b-reversal-10seed
by its own pre-registration.

\textbf{Pre-registration and provenance.} The R2 kill criterion was written before any session ran: the claim fails if, in every arm, the first failure comes only after an explicit error has surfaced in the same session. The gated definition came later, as analysis, and is reported beside the criterion, never in its place. Every follow-up was pre-registered the same way (hypothesis, verdict rule and validity conditions committed before deployment), and we report verdicts in that wording, refutations included. Every number is pasted from a numbers register that one script regenerates from the raw result files; a claim with no verified row is not made. One provenance gap remains: runs before 2026-10-14 did not record the harness file's blob, so their harness identity rests on byte-identical outputs across reruns (identical final text 120/120% reg: R2-qwen3-14b-4096-reconciliation
). Rows are never pooled across platforms, and a configuration flag counts only when the runtime's log shows it applied: an earlier sweep passed a KV-cache precision setting that the runtime silently ignored.

\section{Memory-Architecture Boundaries}\label{sec:4.4}

\textbf{Claim.} The same event, a context window whose KV cache no longer fits the memory the GPU is meant to use, has a different cost shape on each memory architecture: on the AMD APU the server keeps running past the boundary at almost unchanged speed, on the Intel iGPU the server either starts or refuses at one shared-heap limit, and on the discrete GPU the cost is expected to grow with the share of the model pushed out of VRAM. A sizing rule or a router that ignores the architecture is wrong on at least two of the three. Fig.~\ref{fig:FH1} carries this claim; the AMD and Intel panels are measured and the NVIDIA panel is not yet complete.

\textbf{AMD APU: a soft spill, then a hard crash far beyond it.} We ran llama-3.3-70b and qwen3-32b in llama-server with the Vulkan backend and every layer on the GPU (\texttt{-ngl 99}% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
), stepping the window and recording per-process dedicated and shared GPU memory, decode rate and time to first token (TTFT). The platform offers three candidate boundaries: a firmware-reserved dedicated block of 65536 MiB, a Vulkan device-local heap of 76218 MiB, and a Vulkan total of 114327 MiB% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
. We call a point a spill when shared GPU usage grows by more than 1024 MiB over the smallest window% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
. For llama-3.3-70b the last window without spill is 65536 and the first window with spill is 100096, the window the server reported for a request of 100000% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
. At 100096 the process held 1.122 of the dedicated block but only 0.965 of the device-local heap% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
: the onset follows the dedicated block, not the heap the Vulkan driver advertises, so FH1 normalizes the AMD x axis by the dedicated block and says so. Crossing the onset is nearly free. The decode ratio across it is 0.983 and the TTFT ratio 1.004% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
; decode fell from 5.284 $\pm$ 0.004 tok/s at 65536 to 5.196 $\pm$ 0.010 tok/s at 100096, and at 131072, with 18540 MiB more in shared memory than at the smallest window, it was still 5.176 $\pm$ 0.015 tok/s% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
. Nothing in the server's response tells the client it has spilled.

The hard boundary sits much further out and is reached only through clamping. The model's trained context is 131072% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
, and a request above it is served at that value: the last window that started was 221440, clamped to 131072, and the first that failed was 221696, with exit code 3221226505% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
. So the AMD platform has three regimes for this model: it fits, it spills silently at almost no cost (llama-server, Vulkan% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-llama-3.3-70b
), and, for requests far above the trained context, it crashes.

For qwen3-32b no unclamped spill point was measured, so the row has no spill cost% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-qwen3-32b
. In the start-only bisection the last start was 367104, clamped to 131072, and the first failure 367360, with exit code 3221226505% reg: fh1-amd-boundary-qwen3-32b
. A dedicated qwen3-32b spill sweep (llama-server b10970, Vulkan, \texttt{-ngl 99}, 1 warm-up + 5 calls per point) from 20480 to 40960 found no unclamped spill point up to 40960: shared GPU memory grew by at most 20 MiB over 20480, and decode stayed between 11.271 and 11.368 tok/s% reg: fh1-amd-qwen3-32b-spill-sweep
. The qwen3-32b onset is therefore reported as ``above 40960'' for unclamped windows only.

\textbf{Intel iGPU: a hard wall that first-principles accounting predicts.} On the Intel iGPU there is no soft regime: a model either starts or the runtime refuses it. Across five models, the refusal point is consistent with one shared-heap limit L in (48112.7, 48124.0] MiB, 247 MiB above the 47866 MiB budget the driver reports, from 92 lower and 22 upper bounds% reg: A-24-effective-heap-limit
. The limit counts host-visible buffers as well as device memory: accounting for device memory alone is inconsistent, because the largest start (47945.2 MiB) exceeds the smallest refusal (47562.5 MiB)% reg: A-24-effective-heap-limit
. Counting weights, KV cache and compute buffers against the shared heap, with L fitted leave-one-model-out, predicts each model's flip point within -257 to -26 tokens (-0.11\% to -0.01\%), against -2890 to -820 tokens for model file size alone and -1092 to +968 tokens for device-only accounting% reg: A-24-flip-point-prediction
. The flip points span an order of magnitude: (23296, 23552] tokens for llama-3.3-70b and (115712, 115968] for qwen3-32b% reg: A-24-flip-point-per-model
. Two limits apply. These are start tests only, and for several models the flip point lies beyond the trained context, so it is a memory boundary, not a usable window. And the result is for one driver and boot state; we do not claim the 247 MiB margin transfers.

\textbf{NVIDIA discrete GPU: placement measured, cost pending.} With 8188 MiB of VRAM% reg: blade-k1-layer-split
, Ollama keeps llama3.1:8b fully on the GPU at windows of 4096 and 8192 and moves layers to the CPU above them, with VRAM shares of 0.870 at 16384 and 0.656 at 32768; for qwen3:8b the shares are 0.805 and 0.592% reg: blade-k1-layer-split
. The slowdown as a function of the spilled share is not yet in our verified results, so the NVIDIA panel of FH1 is pending.

\section{Runtime Policy}\label{sec:4.5}

\textbf{Claim.} The window an agent actually gets is set by the runtime's policy for the device it detects, not by the model or the memory, and when a prompt outgrows that window Ollama reports success and silently keeps half of it; llama-server at a 4096 window instead fails loudly, with HTTP errors% reg: xruntime-llamacpp-wrong-rate
.

\textbf{Default windows follow the detected device.} Ollama discovers the AMD iGPU through ROCm, with no opt-out, so the AMD APU runs on its iGPU while the Intel laptop, which has no ROCm path, falls through to the CPU% reg: x2-device-detect-mechanism
. The default window follows. On the Intel laptop the runtime loaded 4096 tokens for each of three models, while on the AMD APU it loaded 262144 for qwen3-4b-2507, 131072 for llama3.1:8b and 40960 for qwen3:8b% reg: T2S-vs-X2-default-ctx
, in each case the model's native window (capped only for qwen3:8b% reg: K1v3-X2-table
). The discrete GPU also got 4096 for both models we could measure there% reg: blade-k1-layer-split
. The runtime's own log reports the rule behind the two small values: a default sized from detected VRAM, 4096 for the discrete GPU's 8.0 GiB and 32768 for the Intel iGPU's 36.2 GiB when that iGPU is enabled% reg: gpu-memory-readonly
. Table~\ref{tab:defaults} lists the values.

\textbf{The default decides the outcome.} On the Intel laptop we ran the same agent sessions twice, once as shipped on the CPU and once with the iGPU enabled for the job's own server process. \section{Fixes}\label{sec:4.7}

\textbf{Client-side trimming splits by model, on two vendors.} We pre-registered a client-side fix: before every call the client renders and counts the request with the model's tokenizer and drops whole old turns until the system prompt, the kept turns and the answer budget fit the window with a 5\% % reg: design: harness/x2_r2_client_trim.py
margin, always keeping the system prompt. A model \emph{recovers} when every rule in use is at or above 90\%% reg: design: analysis/r2_mitigation_report.py
, and the hypothesis needed both models to recover at 4096% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-verdict
. Mechanically it worked: 0 context shifts, 0 token cuts, 0 Ollama message drops and 0 exceed-context errors in every cell% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-observed
. The verdict is \emph{refuted}% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-verdict
. On the AMD APU llama3.1:8b recovered at 8192 and 4096% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-verdict
; qwen3:14b recovered at 8192 but not at 4096% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-verdict
, where its \texttt{log\_event} rule fell to 61.7\% (74/120)% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-4096 | paper-percentage-counts
, failing first at turn 3 in every seed (3/3/3% reg: R2-mitigation-v1-4096
). On the Intel laptop's CPU at 4096 % reg: t2s-r2-mitigation-cpu
the split replicates: llama3.1:8b recovered, qwen3:14b did not, again on \texttt{log\_event}, overall refuted% reg: t2s-r2-mitigation-cpu
. The cause is the fix itself: untrimmed, qwen3:14b kept rule 2 on 40/40 turns in every seed% reg: R2-qwen3-14b-4096-reconciliation
. On the first failing trimmed turn its first call requested only \texttt{lookup\_fact}, where the untrimmed run called \texttt{log\_event} and then \texttt{lookup\_fact}% reg: R2-qwen3-14b-4096-reconciliation

turns besides the system prompt, so trimming removes the in-context examples of the two-call pattern the model imitates.

\textbf{A router does not dominate.} We pre-registered a comparison of eight % reg: design: harness/policy_compare.py

Under the simulated cap of Section~\ref{sec:4.5} the router joined the frontier in all four capped cells, and the verdict stays refuted only because client trimming did too% reg: b5-sim-constraint

\textbf{Recovering after a silent failure on Ollama is costly and incomplete.} A cloud model given the whole client-side history late, on every wrong task of the protocol-check task run (Section~\ref{sec:4.6a}), fixed 84.8\% (167/197) of them, task-level 95\% Wilson CI [79.1\%, 89.1\%]% reg: cloud-redo-full-context

Deciding at session start instead, with the rule ``run locally if local accuracy at the recommended setting is within a tolerance of the cloud model's'', no measured device qualifies at matched quality: the AMD APU at its best setting is 7.8 points below the small cloud model and 11.1 below the mid-tier one, and the Intel iGPU, measured by session pass rate rather than task accuracy, is 16.7 and 20.0 points below% reg: hybrid-route-at-start
. At matched quality the measured answer is therefore the cloud from the start; step-level escalation stays a narrow middle case, since window overflow does not pick out the wrong steps (Section~\ref{sec:4.7p}).

\subsection{Predictor}\label{sec:4.7p}

\textbf{The runtime's context-shift trigger, characterized.} The step that discards the system prompt at the smallest window is llama.cpp's context shift (Section~\ref{sec:4.6b}), and its trigger can be read from the llama.cpp source rather than fitted: a shift happens on a call when the prompt left after the message-level trim, plus the tokens generated, exceeds the loaded window. We scored that rule on the AMD mechanism sessions, one per tier, against the shifts the server log recorded. At 4096 it gave TP 34 FP 0 FN 0 TN 46, and at 32768 TP 10 FP 0 FN 0 TN 70, precision and recall 100.0\% in both (34/34 at 4096, 10/10 at 32768), with misses 0 over all tiers% reg: R2-shift-predictor-confusion
. The exact form matters, which is evidence that the rule is the mechanism and not a correlate: P+C$>$n\_ctx gives TP 44 FP 0 FN 0, while the prompt alone, P$\geq$n\_ctx-1, gives TP 0 FP 0 FN 44% reg: R2-shift-predictor-confusion
. The generated count is not known before the call, so a client substitutes the generation budget: P+num\_predict$>$n\_ctx gives TP 44 FP 22 FN 0% reg: R2-shift-predictor-confusion
: this characterizes the runtime's rule as it can be evaluated before the call, missing no shift at the cost of false alarms; it is not a flag we contribute. It has no tunable parameter, but it was built and scored on the same sessions; no held-out observed sessions exist yet.

\textbf{It predicts the shift, not the failure.} Applied to the real run as a prediction only, where no server log exists, the rule flags 102/240 calls for llama3.1:8b at 4096 and 0/240 for qwen3:14b% reg: R2-shift-predicted-real-v1
, yet qwen3:14b also loses rules at 4096. For that model the loss runs through what the message-level trim removes, not through the shift% reg: R2-qwen3-14b-4096-reconciliation
. What the trim leaves at the front of the window is usually a fragment: at 4096, 80.3\% (61/76) of truncated calls began with an orphaned message% reg: R2-orphan-rates
.

\textbf{Window overflow is not a general wrong-answer detector.} We measured the flag ``this task turn's prompt exceeded the loaded window'' as a predictor of the outcome ``the task ended wrong'' on the protocol-check task run of Section~\ref{sec:4.6a} (qwen3:14b, AMD APU)% reg: impact-v3-detection-value-measured
. At the runtime default no turn overflowed, and the flag missed 17/17 wrong tasks (recall 0.0\% (0/17)). At 16384 it reached precision 15.4\% (4/26) and recall 21.1\% (4/19); at 8192 precision 45.3\% (48/106) and recall 90.6\% (48/53); at 4096 recall 100.0\% (63/63) but precision 42.9\% (63/147), flagging 147 of 150 turns (TP 63 FP 84)% reg: impact-v3-detection-value-measured
. Where the window is large, wrong answers have other causes that the flag cannot see; where it is small, nearly every step overflows and the flag no longer separates good steps from bad. The shift rule and the window flag explain system-prompt loss; they are not contributed detectors or a cheap escalation filter.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:4.8}

\textbf{Platforms and versions.} We measure one machine per vendor, so a platform effect cannot be separated from that machine's driver and memory configuration. Agent runs use Ollama 0.34.4 % reg: R2-qwen3-14b-4096-reconciliation
and direct runs llama.cpp b10970% reg: design: docs/HARDWARE.md (version record; no register row, gap G13)
; default windows and trimming are runtime code and can change in any release. The NVIDIA laptop contributes validation and mechanism sessions only, because its validation gate held back the real agent runs% reg: blade-r2-gate-v2
, and the router and context-policy comparison ran on the AMD APU alone% reg: b5-verdict
, whose default window is the model's native one.

\textbf{Statistics and provenance.} Cells have three % reg: H-scale-verdict
to five % reg: R2-v1b-gated-per-model
seeds, ten for qwen3:8b% reg: R2-qwen3-8b-reversal-10seed
. A 0/5 or 5/5 cell has a point bootstrap interval that understates uncertainty, and the scale and family results rest on three sessions per cell. Sampling uses temperature 0 % reg: design: harness/x2_r2_agent.py
and a fixed request seed, so seeds vary the session script, not the sampling; nonzero temperatures are untested. Where a script times or scores repeated calls it follows one warm-up call plus N measured calls (N = 3 for the KV-precision recall check% reg: b5-kv-recall-f16-rerun
, whose needles within a call are not independent, so that check is descriptive); session-time comparisons are over three seeds% reg: cost-cut-config-time
. Runs before 2026-10-14 rest on byte-identical reruns for their harness identity (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}).

\textbf{Workload.} The R2 rules are synthetic and checkable; deployed system prompts differ. Guardrail rules 6 to 8 (privacy, refusal, confirmation) already fail at full context for both models tested, for example rule 6 at 0.417 and 0.250 of bait turns% reg: x2-evalset-val-v1-gates
, so they are excluded from every window measurement and support no claim here. The tools-on-every-call task workload rests on qwen3:14b alone, under the deviation of Section~\ref{sec:4.6a}; qwen3:8b, llama3.1:8b, mistral-small3.2:24b and llama3.3:70b failed its validation% reg: x2-evalset-tasks-val-v2-qwen3-8b-gates | x2-evalset-tasks-real-v2-wrong-rate | x2-evalset-tasks-val-v2-mistral-small32-gates | x2-evalset-tasks-val-v2-llama33-70b-gates
. B3 task accuracy therefore has one valid model; the cross-model results are R2 rule-following evidence, not B3 task accuracy.

\textbf{Cost figures.} Cloud cost per real agent task is priced from traces, not executed% reg: cloud-cost-per-real-task
, and its caching case is an assumption. Our first cloud redo of wrong local tasks was a broken protocol: 109 % reg: cloud-redo-diagnosis
of its 118 % reg: cloud-redo-diagnosis
failures were turns where the model's default thinking spent the whole 384-token % reg: cloud-redo-diagnosis
output budget and returned an empty answer, so its fix rate measured the token budget, not the cloud model. We cite no fix rate or cost from it; the corrected full-history redo (thinking limited to between tool calls, 2048 output tokens per call against the local run's 384) is the one reported in Section~\ref{sec:4.7}% reg: cloud-redo-full-context
.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:4.9}

On Ollama, local LLM agents lose context without telling anyone. On every platform we measured, Ollama trims, shifts or cuts a conversation that outgrows the window, and the request still succeeds. A stock llama-server at a 4096 window instead fails loudly, with an HTTP error on every wrong task turn, while the 4096 effect on wrong tasks replicates there (+92.0 points, 95\% CI +88.7 to +94.7)% reg: xruntime-llamacpp-wrong-rate
. Rule loss appears in the 8B and 14B models (5 seeds) and in qwen3:32b (2 of 3 sessions at 4096)% reg: H-scale-verdict
, and not in llama3.3:70b or mistral-small3.2:24b (0 of 3 each)% reg: H-scale-verdict | H-scale-family-verdict
. History loss appears in all of them% reg: H-scale-verdict | H-scale-family-verdict
. These cross-model results are R2 rule-following evidence, not B3 task accuracy, which rests on qwen3:14b alone. The window is often not the user's choice: the runtime picks it from the detected device, and an unmodified install on an Intel CPU ran agents at 4096 tokens% reg: t2s-r2-real-cpu
. That is the window at which, on the AMD APU, qwen3:14b ended 42.0\% (63/150) of tasks in a wrong state against 11.3\% (17/150) at its default, all silently on Ollama 0.34.4% reg: x2-evalset-tasks-real-v2-wrong-rate
.

For practitioners, five lessons follow. Set the window explicitly for each device and verify the loaded value% reg: window-override-methods
. Surface the runtime's own cut-off log lines~\cite{ollama14259}, but do not treat them as a correctness check. Try a quantized KV cache before a smaller window: on the AMD APU it cut session time by 85\% (37.1 of 43.7 min) and 65\% (139.4 of 214.8 min, 3 sessions per setting) with no measured loss of rules or recall% reg: cost-cut-config-time | b5-kv-recall-f16-rerun
. Treat trimming as a change to the prompt, since dropping old turns also drops the examples the model imitates% reg: R2-qwen3-14b-4096-reconciliation
. Validate an agent on the backend it will run on, because identical model files and settings behaved differently on CUDA, Vulkan and ROCm% reg: blade-r2-gate-v2 | x2-r2-llama-val-32k-vulkan | x2-r2-llama-val-32k-offload
.

Future work follows from what is pending. The router and context-policy comparison must run on the Intel and NVIDIA laptops, whose default windows are small enough that loss occurs% reg: b5-verdict
. A hybrid deployment needs a general verifier for wrong answers produced inside the window: a late cloud redo with the full history fixed 84.8\% (167/197) of wrong tasks % reg: cloud-redo-full-context
at 1.76x the cost of a fresh cloud run of the same task% reg: late-escalation-cost-vs-fresh
, and a plain redo could not undo executed actions; on simulated tools, cascaded rollback fixed 28/28 (195/197 overall)% reg: own-rollback-redo | cloud-rescue-combined
, a stand-in in which earlier wrong turns are replaced by reference calls, so a live rollback remains future work. More models, seeds and runtime versions would turn the model-dependence of rule loss from an observation into a predictable property.
